# Analysis of Learning Objectives: *Nanotechnological Physics* (4014971)

**Document reviewed:** Course specification (TQF 3) for *Nanotechnological Physics* (4014971), Physics program.  
**Scope of this analysis:** The stated end-of-course learning results, the course description, the TQF outcome mapping, the weekly teaching plan, and the assessment plan.

## Executive assessment

The course has a **sound conceptual intent**: it combines foundational nanotechnology knowledge, analysis, inquiry, laboratory-oriented work, quantitative/data skills, communication, and responsible participation. Its weekly topics—nanoscale measurement, nanomaterial synthesis, instrumentation, characterization, and applications—are appropriate for a Nanotechnological Physics course.[1]

The objectives, however, are **not yet a fully coherent and assessable set of course learning outcomes (CLOs)**. The primary statements are very broad and largely refer to “physics” rather than the specific nanotechnology competences taught. Several intended outcomes are not formally mapped to the matching TQF domains, while practical laboratory competence and the use of nanotechnology for learning management are stated in the course description but not expressed as assessable outcomes. Most importantly, the two “objectives for course development” refer to **plasma physics and plasma technology**, which conflicts with the title, description, teaching plan, and assessments for nanotechnology.[1]

| Overall dimension | Judgement | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Disciplinary relevance | **Strong** | The course content is centred on foundational and applied nanotechnology topics. |
| Breadth of intended learning | **Strong** | Knowledge, analysis, problem-solving, responsibility, quantitative work, communication, and technology use are all represented. |
| Objective specificity | **Needs improvement** | The statements do not name the nanoscale concepts, methods, instruments, outputs, or performance conditions expected. |
| Measurability | **Needs improvement** | Success criteria and direct evidence for several outcomes are not defined. |
| Constructive alignment | **Partially aligned** | Teaching activities and assessment exist, but some outcome-to-domain and outcome-to-assessment links are incomplete or inconsistent. |
| Internal document consistency | **Critical correction required** | The development-objective section names plasma physics/technology instead of nanotechnology. |

## 1. What the document currently treats as learning objectives

After instruction, the document states that students should understand physics principles and explain phenomena; analyse using the physics learned; analyse problems and propose creative, systematic solutions; be responsible for individual and group work; and use statistical or mathematical techniques for inquiry and problem solving.[1] These are the clearest **end-of-course learning results** and form the core of this analysis.

The course description adds a more discipline-specific intention. It expects students to explain, analyse, and produce integrated work concerning nanotechnology’s meaning and scope, nanoscale measurement, nanomaterial synthesis, synthesis and characterization instruments, and applications. It also specifies inquiry and practical operations for eventual use in learning management.[1] The TQF table then formally maps the course to ethics/responsibility (1.2), knowledge (2.1, 2.2, 2.4), and numerical/data, communication, and information-technology skills (5.1, 5.2).[1]

| Current stated outcome | Predominant cognitive or behavioural level | Evidence of good intent | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Understand physics principles and explain phenomena | Understanding → explaining | Requires students to articulate concepts, not merely recall them. | It is generic “physics”; it does not identify nanoscale phenomena or concepts. |
| Analyse using learned physics principles | Analysis | Signals higher-order reasoning. | No phenomenon, data set, problem type, or expected analytic product is stated. |
| Analyse problems and propose creative, systematic solutions while considering theory and consequences | Analysis → evaluation → solution design | Incorporates theory, consequences, creativity, and systematic reasoning. | It has no stated performance context, decision criteria, or assessment product. |
| Take responsibility for individual and group work | Affective / professional behaviour | Appropriate for laboratory and collaborative work. | The observable behaviours and scoring criteria are absent; the formal interpersonal domain is left blank. |
| Use statistical or mathematical techniques for inquiry and problem solving | Application → analysis | Connects quantitative tools to scientific investigation. | The particular techniques, data tasks, accuracy standards, and evidence are not specified. |

> **Interpretive point:** The verbs “explain,” “analyse,” “propose,” and “use” are potentially observable. Their value is weakened here because the document does not identify the **content**, **conditions**, **product**, or **standard** that would show successful performance.

## 2. Alignment with content, TQF outcomes, teaching, and assessment

There is a reasonable high-level match between the syllabus and the aims. Weeks 1–15 address nanotechnology foundations, nanoscale measurement, nanomaterial synthesis, synthesis instrumentation, material characterization, and applications through inquiry/practical work.[1] The course also allocates 30 lecture hours and 30 practical hours, which supports an applied scientific-learning intention.[1]

Nevertheless, formal alignment is incomplete. The document’s broader learning result about analysis and creative problem solving fits an intellectual-skills domain, yet the TQF intellectual-skills section (Domain 3) shows no selected outcome, teaching strategy, or assessment. Likewise, individual/group responsibility is a stated result, but the TQF interpersonal-relationships-and-responsibility section (Domain 4) is blank; responsibility is instead assessed within ethics (1.2). The statistical/mathematical outcome maps more plausibly to Domain 5.1, but its 30% assessment is a presentation and question-answer activity, not clearly a quantitative analysis task or laboratory report.[1]

|---|---|---|---|
| Explain nanotechnology-related phenomena | Nanotechnology foundations and lectures/practical activities are provided. | Knowledge outcomes 2.1 and 2.2 are assessed through midterm/final examinations (60%). | **Partial to strong**, but the written outcome should name nanotechnology explicitly. |
| Analyse physics problems and propose solutions | Inquiry, discussion, applications, and practical work are described. | Intellectual-skills Domain 3 is blank; no specific analytical artefact is named. | **Weak formal alignment.** |
| Demonstrate responsibility in individual and group work | Participation, group inquiry, and assigned work are mentioned. | Ethics 1.2 is observed weekly (10%); Domain 4 is blank. | **Partial alignment**, but classification and rubric need correction. |
| Apply statistics/mathematics to scientific inquiry | The course states data analysis and information-technology activities. | Domain 5.1 is selected; presentation/Q&A carry 30%. | **Partial alignment**, but direct calculation/data interpretation evidence is unclear. |
| Communicate findings using digital tools | Group research and classroom presentations are planned. | Domain 5.2 is selected and presentation/Q&A are assessed. | **Moderate alignment**, provided a presentation rubric is used. |
| Carry out practical/inquiry work with nano materials or data | The description names practical operations; the syllabus contains practical teaching. | No explicit practical-performance outcome, laboratory rubric, or lab-report assessment is identified. | **Major missing outcome/assessment link.** |

## 3. Strengths of the existing objectives

First, the objectives have a **broad competency orientation**. They do not limit the course to content acquisition: they include analysis, solution development, responsibility, quantitative reasoning, communication, and technology use. This is appropriate for a course with both theory and practical hours.[1]

Second, the teaching strategies are varied. Lectures are supplemented with visual/online media, inquiry, group research, presentation, discussion, everyday-life cases, and practical activities. These strategies could support active learning if their tasks and rubrics are made more explicit.[1]

Third, the scheduled topics produce a logical disciplinary sequence. Students first encounter the field and its scale, then measurement, synthesis, instrumentation, characterization, and applications. This sequence can support progressively more complex performance outcomes.[1]

## 4. Principal weaknesses and risks

| Priority | Issue | Why it matters | Recommended action |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1 — Critical** | The course-development objectives mention plasma physics and plasma technology. | This is inconsistent with a nanotechnology course and could create an accreditation, quality-assurance, and curriculum-communication error. | Replace both statements with nanotechnology-specific development objectives and verify all related sections for copied text. |
| **2 — High** | The core outcomes use generic physics language rather than nanotechnology language. | The teaching plan is nano-specific, but the objectives do not tell students which discipline-specific competence they must demonstrate. | Name nanoscale concepts, synthesis, characterization, measurement, applications, and data interpretation directly. |
| **3 — High** | Analytical and problem-solving outcomes have no TQF Domain 3 mapping. | Intended higher-order learning is not formally represented in the outcome matrix or assessment design. | Populate the appropriate intellectual-skills outcome(s), teaching activities, and evidence. |
| **4 — High** | Practical/laboratory competence is implied but not assessed as a learning outcome. | Thirty practical hours should yield observable scientific practice—e.g., safe procedure, data recording, instrument selection, or interpretation. | Add a practical/inquiry CLO and assess it with a lab notebook/report, practical check, or structured investigation. |
| **5 — Medium** | Quantitative/data analysis is assessed mainly by presentation/Q&A. | Presentation alone may not show accurate calculations, graphical interpretation, uncertainty awareness, or use of data. | Require a graded data-analysis component within a lab report, problem set, or case study. |
| **6 — Medium** | Responsibility is measured by general observation without published criteria. | Students and assessors may interpret “responsibility” inconsistently. | Use a short analytic rubric for preparation, task completion, contribution, academic integrity, and collaboration. |
| **7 — Medium** | Several assessment methods in the weekly plan are informal observation, questions, and discussion. | These can support formative feedback but provide limited reproducible evidence for complex outcomes. | Retain them as formative checks and add rubric-scored products for summative decisions. |

## 5. Recommended revision of the course learning objectives

The following is an **illustrative revision**, not a replacement for program-level approval. It preserves the document’s intent while making the expected performances more nano-specific and assessable.

| Proposed CLO | Observable performance and scope | Suitable direct evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **CLO 1. Explain nanoscale principles.** | Explain the meaning, scale-dependent properties, and selected physical phenomena of nanotechnology using correct scientific terminology. | Short-answer/problem-based examination questions and concept explanation. |
| **CLO 2. Select and interpret measurement/characterization methods.** | Select an appropriate nanoscale measurement or characterization method for a stated material/question and interpret the resulting qualitative or quantitative evidence. | Instrument-selection case, annotated result/data interpretation, or lab report. |
| **CLO 3. Analyse nanomaterial synthesis approaches.** | Compare suitable nanomaterial-synthesis approaches and justify a basic procedure in terms of purpose, variables, limitations, and safe practice. | Design brief, comparison matrix, or structured problem task. |
| **CLO 4. Conduct inquiry and analyse data.** | Use appropriate mathematical, statistical, or graphical methods to analyse nano-related experimental or provided data and draw conclusions consistent with the evidence. | Data-analysis worksheet and laboratory/inquiry report. |
| **CLO 5. Evaluate nanotechnology applications.** | Evaluate a selected nanotechnology application by connecting scientific principles with benefits, limitations, and relevant consequences. | Evidence-based case analysis and presentation. |
| **CLO 6. Communicate and work responsibly.** | Communicate findings clearly with appropriate digital tools and demonstrate reliable, ethical collaboration in individual and group scientific work. | Presentation rubric, team contribution record, and responsibility rubric. |
| **Optional CLO 7. Design a learning application.** | Design a concise learning activity that accurately applies an aspect of nanotechnology, if “use in learning management” is a required program outcome. | Lesson/activity design with content-accuracy rubric. |

These outcomes should be adapted to the expected student level and available laboratory facilities. Each should be assessed at least once through a **direct measure**; participation or attendance observations should complement, rather than substitute for, evidence of scientific competence.

## 6. Suggested assessment realignment

The current assessment allocation is 10% behaviour/responsibility observation, 60% midterm and final examinations for knowledge outcomes, and 30% classroom presentation and Q&A for Domain 5 outcomes.[1] This weighting can be retained in broad form, but the evidence should more directly match the revised outcomes.

| Assessment component | Indicative weighting | CLOs directly evidenced | Minimum rubric or criteria |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Conceptual and problem-based examinations | 40% | CLO 1, parts of CLOs 2–3 | Correct concepts, reasoning, appropriate terminology, justified answer. |
| Instrument/data interpretation task | 15% | CLO 2, CLO 4 | Appropriate method selection, correct reading of evidence, accuracy of analysis. |
| Practical inquiry notebook and report | 20% | CLO 3, CLO 4, CLO 6 | Procedure/record quality, data integrity, analysis, conclusions, safety/ethics. |
| Nano-application case presentation | 15% | CLO 5, CLO 6 | Evidence use, analysis of benefits/limitations/consequences, clarity, response to questions. |
| Individual and group responsibility rubric | 10% | CLO 6 | Preparation, contribution, completion, integrity, constructive collaboration. |

## Conclusion

The document describes a potentially valuable, practice-oriented nanotechnology course. Its learning aims are broad enough to support scientific knowledge, inquiry, quantitative reasoning, and communication, and the teaching plan is mostly consistent with this direction.[1] To become clear, auditable, and learner-centred, the specification should first correct the plasma-technology text, then rewrite the learning objectives around specific nano-related performances, explicitly map analytical and teamwork outcomes to the appropriate TQF domains, and introduce direct rubric-based evidence for practical and quantitative competence. These revisions would make the course’s intended learning both easier for students to understand and easier for the program to demonstrate.
